I am an incoming MSc Economics student at the LSE, and I completed my undergraduate degree in economics at the Madras School of Economics. My interests span economics, mathematics, and political philosophy.

In economics, I am particularly drawn to macroeconomics. My undergraduate projects concentrated on empirical work — testing the validity of the Phillips curve in India, and modelling sin taxation in India — alongside an expository project breaking down Hamilton's seminal 1989 paper on regime-switching Markov models used to describe business cycles.

At MSE, I was a Teaching Assistant for three courses: Calculus I, Mathematical Statistics, and Intermediate Macroeconomics.

Aside from coursework, I independently studied complex analysis using Brown and Churchill's textbook as a guide — I've worked through the first six chapters, up to residues and poles — and have prepared an expository document developing the understanding of the Riemann Hypothesis.

In political philosophy, I have read Locke, Hobbes, and Rousseau. Some thoughts about democracy and Rousseau's views on it, while I was reading The Social Contract, prompted me to write a short essay on the subject.

Outside academics, I grew up playing cricket, and I watch football every weekend. I support Tottenham Hotspur.

Understanding the Riemann Hypothesis

A step-by-step development, from the Euler product through analytic continuation, the Gamma function, the theta identity, and the completed zeta function and its functional equation, ending with the zero-free line Re(s) = 1 and an expository section linking the Riemann Hypothesis to the Prime Number Theorem. Written after independent study of complex analysis from Brown and Churchill, with Stein and Shakarchi as the main reference for the final two sections.

2026 · Political philosophy

Rousseau and modern India: eight decades of a large democracy

An essay set against Rousseau's argument in The Social Contract that democracy requires a small, homogeneous, virtuous people. India is none of these, and the piece argues that its Constitution and independent institutions have done work Rousseau did not foresee — while its civil service has behaved much as he warned an unaccountable administrative class would.
